Legal & financial documents

Structured settlement documents, explained

A structured settlement isn't one document — it's a small stack of them, each doing a different legal job, drafted so precisely that changing a single verb can shift who owes the money for the next forty years. This guide walks through the stack the way an editor would: what each document is for, what its key language does, and which details deserve a second read. It's part of this site's series on reading and understanding professional documents.

First, the shape of the deal

In a structured settlement, an injury claim resolves not as a single lump sum but as a schedule of future periodic payments — monthly checks, annual amounts, lump sums at milestone dates, or a mix. Federal tax law is what makes the arrangement attractive: damages for personal physical injuries are excluded from income under Internal Revenue Code § 104(a)(2) whether paid as a lump sum or as periodic payments, and since the Periodic Payment Settlement Tax Act of 1982, the periodic-payment version enjoys statutory certainty. The catch that shapes every document below: the recipient must not have actual or constructive receipt of the underlying fund. The paperwork exists largely to keep that true.

If payments are ever sold: the transfer stack

A second, entirely different set of documents appears if the payment recipient later sells some or all future payments to a factoring company for cash today. Congress and every state legislature decided this transaction is risky enough to wrap in mandatory paperwork: IRC § 5891 imposes a 40% excise tax on the buyer unless the transfer is approved in advance by a court order under a state structured settlement protection act (SSPA). That makes the documents below effectively universal:

  • The disclosure statement. Delivered to the seller before signing, typically listing the payments being sold, their aggregate value, the discounted present value (computed against a published federal rate), the gross purchase price, itemized fees, and the effective discount rate. This is the document that states, in one place, what the deal actually costs. State law dictates its timing and contents.
  • The transfer agreement. The contract of sale — which specific payments, for how much, with what representations. Read for what happens if the court declines approval, and for any provisions that reach payments beyond the ones being sold.
  • The transfer petition. The filing that asks a court to approve the transfer. SSPAs require the court to find, among other things, that the transfer is in the best interest of the payee, taking account of the welfare and support of dependents.
  • The qualified court order. The approval order that satisfies § 5891 and shields the transaction from the excise tax. No order, no lawful transfer — and courts do say no: in one 2026 Pennsylvania federal decision, approval was denied where the pricing worked out to an effective annual interest rate near 24%, which the court found could not be in the payee's best interest.
  • The assignment to a new holder. Approved payments are frequently assigned once more, to an investor who buys the right to receive the remaining schedule. The paperwork at that stage restates the same facts a third time — issuing carrier, payment dates and amounts, and the order that approved the transfer — which is why published secondary-market payment-stream listings are a practical place to see a completed stack summarised in plain terms.

The federal-settlement special case

When the paying party is the United States — most commonly a Federal Tort Claims Act settlement — the same document stack acquires extra layers, and one structural difference dominates: the government often owns the annuity and retains the right to designate the payee. Courts have held that a state court cannot order the government to redirect those payments — the Sixth Circuit's TransAmerica decision (2007) treated that as a matter of federal sovereign immunity — which means the ordinary transfer stack above simply doesn't work against a government-owned annuity. Other federal particulars visible in the documents: settlements above a threshold require Justice Department approval; FTCA attorney fees are capped by statute (25% of a judgment or settlement, 20% of an administrative award); and the brokers who arrange annuities for federal settlements appear on a Justice Department list maintained under 28 C.F.R. § 50.24, renewed annually by sworn declaration.

An editor's checklist for reading the stack

  1. Trace one payment through every document. Pick a single scheduled payment and confirm its amount, date, and payee are identical in the settlement agreement, the qualified assignment, the annuity contract, and the benefits letter. Discrepancies among these documents are exactly the kind of defect that surfaces decades later.
  2. Find the verb that creates the obligation. "Shall pay" and "shall purchase an annuity" are different promises with different survivors.
  3. Identify the owner of every contract. Payee ≠ owner, and only owners can act.
  4. In any transfer document, find the effective discount rate — not the fees, not the "purchase price," the rate. It is the single number that makes offers comparable.
  5. Check the definitions section against usage. These documents chain-reference each other ("the Periodic Payments," "the Qualified Assignment"); a defined term used loosely in one document weakens the whole chain. The same discipline applies to any templated legal document set.

Scope note: this is a document-literacy guide, not legal or financial advice. Structured settlement transfers are governed by the protection act of the payee's state plus IRC § 5891, and the terms above vary by state and by contract — anyone considering a transfer should have the actual documents reviewed by their own counsel or advisor.
