The craft

Technical editing and document formatting, demystified

"Can you look this over?" hides at least four different jobs, priced differently and done in a different order. Knowing the levels of edit — and asking for the right one — is the difference between a document that gets polished and one that gets rescued.

The levels of edit

LevelWhat the editor doesWhen you need it
ProofreadingFinal pass on near-final pages: typos, punctuation, broken cross-references, bad line and page breaks. No rewriting.After layout, before the document ships or prints.
CopyeditingSentence-level correctness and consistency: grammar, usage, terminology, capitalization, numbers, citations — applied against a style guide.Every document that leaves the building. This is the default meaning of "editing."
Substantive / line editingRewriting for clarity and flow: untangling sentences, cutting repetition, smoothing transitions, flagging logic gaps. Common for multi-author reports and ESL authors.When the content is right but the reading is hard.
Structural / developmental editingReorganizing the document itself: what goes where, what's missing, what should be an appendix, whether the executive summary answers the reader's question.Early — before the writing is "done." Cheapest bought first, most expensive bought last.

Formatting is the separate, parallel craft: making the document's typography, numbering, tables, and figures consistent and navigable — ideally by rebuilding it on a proper style-based template rather than patching by hand.

How editors price work

Professional technical editors typically charge by the hour, and throughput depends on the level of edit: a copyedit of reasonably clean technical prose commonly runs a handful of pages per hour, while heavy line editing or ESL rewriting can run much slower. That is why an experienced editor will ask to see the document before quoting — the honest unit of estimation is the sample page, not the page count. Flat per-project rates are common for repeat work like newsletters and standing reports, where the effort is predictable.

Two practical implications when you're buying:

  • Send instructions with the document — the deadline, the style guide (or "match this earlier report"), what must not be touched, and the RFP or client instructions if compliance matters.
  • Ask for tracked changes. In Word, an edit delivered with track changes and queries in comments is auditable; a silently "fixed" file is not. You should be able to see and veto every change.

What good editors ask you

  1. Who is the reader, and what decision do they make with this document?
  2. Which style guide governs — company guide, client guide, or a standard one (Chicago, APA, GPO, your agency's)?
  3. What level of edit do you actually want — and is the schedule consistent with it?
  4. Is the formatting in scope, and is there a real template to format onto?
  5. Are there compliance constraints (page limits, font floors, required sections) that override style?

If you're hiring and the editor asks none of these, keep looking.

Editing AI-drafted text

A growing share of business documents now start life as machine-generated drafts. They arrive grammatical but generic: confident filler, invented specifics, and a voice that belongs to nobody. Editing them is closer to substantive editing than proofreading — verify every fact and citation, cut the throat-clearing, and rewrite until the document sounds like the organization that signs it. The levels-of-edit discipline above applies unchanged; only the failure modes are new.
